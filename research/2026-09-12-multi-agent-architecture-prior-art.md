# Prior Art: Single-Decider-With-Advisors vs. Peer-to-Peer Multi-Agent Architecture

Date: 2026-09-12
Scope: literature/prior-art review, not implementation. No DFHack/DF-specific code was touched; this is pure research to inform the `openclaw` multi-agent-by-task decomposition that `Working.md`/`ROADMAP.md` (2026-09-12 rows) flag as the next concrete build after the MCP seam.
Status: research spec. Cited claims are marked with confidence; nothing here has been tested against this project's own fortress.

## 0. The problem, restated, and why it's not new

Restated domain-neutrally, per this repo's own rule: **a single long-horizon control problem over one shared, slowly-mutating world state, serviced by several specialised advisors who analyse state and propose actions, arbitrated and executed by one actor**, with full auditability of who recommended what and why, learning from outcomes over time, bounded cost, and urgent cases handled by precommitted reflexes rather than deliberation.

This is a much older problem than LLM agents. It is the exact shape of 1970s AI blackboard systems, incident command, military mission command, medical multidisciplinary teams, and dispatch/scheduling operations research — and, in the last three years, it has also become an active empirical question in the LLM multi-agent literature, which is good news: this section doesn't have to rely on 50-year-old AI folklore alone, there is now real quantitative evidence about the exact architectural choice this project is making.

The short answer to the load-bearing question (§1) is: **the v1 ban on specialist-to-specialist chat is well-founded, not superstition, and the strongest evidence for it is 2025-2026 vintage, not analogy.** The rest of this document is the trail.

## 1. Single-decider-with-advisors vs. peer-to-peer agent chat

### 1.1 What the strongest available evidence shows

**Cognition AI's "Don't Build Multi-Agents"** (Walden Yan, 2025, cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents) is the most direct, plainly-stated version of this project's exact concern, from a production agent company (Devin), not an academic paper. Its argument, read in full:

- Parallel/peer multi-agent setups fail because **subagents act on incomplete information and make conflicting implicit decisions** — their own example: one subagent building a Mario-style background while a sibling builds an incompatible bird, with no shared context to catch the mismatch before it ships.
- Two principles they say multi-agent systems routinely violate: **share full context** (not just a task description — full agent traces) and **avoid splitting decision-making in ways that can conflict**, because "actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results."
- Their prescribed fix is a **single-threaded linear agent** for most work, with a compression/summarization step for context-window overflow on long tasks — explicitly flagged as still hard to get right.
- Their closing position: real multi-agent *collaboration* (as opposed to hierarchical delegation) needs cross-agent communication advances that "don't yet exist at scale" — i.e., not solved, not almost solved.

Confidence: this is a single company's production experience and blog-post argumentation, not a controlled study — treat it as strong practitioner testimony, not as a result with error bars. But it is testimony from a team that shipped and then walked back a peer-to-peer design, which is a different and more informative kind of evidence than a lab benchmark.

**Anthropic's own "How we built our multi-agent research system"** (engineering blog, also summarized independently at claude.com/blog/building-multi-agent-systems-when-and-how-to-use-them) is the closest thing to a real deployed system's postmortem, and it is a *single-decider-with-workers* architecture, not peer-to-peer, by explicit design: a Lead Researcher orchestrator spawns subagents, each with its own context window, that report back to the orchestrator only — subagents do not talk to each other. Reported numbers:
- **90.2% improvement over single-agent Claude Opus 4** on their internal research eval, when multi-agent is used for the right kind of task (parallelisable research).
- **~15x the token cost of a single chat interaction**, and separately, **token usage alone explained ~80% of the variance in performance** across their eval runs — i.e., most of the gain is bought with tokens, not architecture cleverness.
- Explicitly scoped: multi-agent helps for tasks decomposable into *independent parallel strands*; it is **worse** for "tightly interdependent tasks such as coding" where subtask outputs constrain each other.
- Real failure modes they report fixing by hand over weeks: context loss at handoffs, agents spending more tokens coordinating than doing the work, and verification subagents prematurely marking failing outputs as passing.

Confidence: this is a first-party production account with real numbers, but it is Anthropic's own system on Anthropic's own eval — not independently replicated, and "improvement over single-agent" is specific to their research-QA task shape, not a general law. Still, it is a second, independent production team (after Cognition) converging on hierarchical orchestrator-to-worker with **no worker-to-worker channel**, for a reason that generalizes past their specific product: workers making mutually-invisible decisions is the actual failure mechanism, not a detail of either company's stack.

**Multi-agent debate research** (Du et al. 2023, the originating paper, plus more recent failure-mode work) is the academic evidence, and it complicates a naive "more agents talking = better" story rather than supporting either extreme cleanly:
- Du et al.'s original multi-agent debate (arXiv, 2023) does show debate improving factual accuracy and reducing hallucination on arithmetic/reasoning benchmarks over single-model self-correction, across multiple rounds of agents seeing each other's answers and revising.
- But **"Talk Isn't Always Cheap: Understanding Failure Modes in Multi-Agent Debate"** (arXiv:2509.05396, 2025) reports the opposite direction of failure specifically for peer-to-peer *exchange between equals*: agents exhibit **sycophancy and conformity**, shifting from correct to incorrect answers to agree with a peer rather than defending correct reasoning, correct-to-incorrect transitions occurring *more often* than incorrect-to-correct ones in their runs, with measured accuracy drops (e.g., MMLU one mixed-model configuration fell 8.2 points post-debate; CommonSenseQA fell 1.2-5.0 points). Notably, the paper's own baselines are single-agent performance and majority vote — **it does not compare against a single stronger arbitrator/verifier model**, which is a real gap in that specific study, flagged so as not to overstate what it proves; but the finding that stands on its own (peers converging toward agreement rather than toward correctness) is exactly the diffusion-of-responsibility-flavoured risk a peer-chat design invites.

Reconciling these: debate-among-equals can help when the task is "many independent attempts, pick/blend the best" (Du et al.'s framing) and can actively hurt when it's "peers negotiate a single joint answer under time/social pressure" (the 2025 failure-mode paper's framing) — the second framing is structurally closer to what "specialist advisors talking to each other" would look like in this project (a construction advisor and a military advisor hashing out a shared plan) than the first.

**MAST — "Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?"** (Cemri et al., arXiv:2503.13657, NeurIPS 2025 poster) is the most rigorous single source found: a taxonomy built from 150 human-annotated traces (inter-annotator kappa = 0.88, i.e. genuinely reliable annotation, not one person's judgment calls), scaled to 1,600+ traces across 7 popular multi-agent frameworks via an LLM-as-judge pipeline calibrated against the human labels. 14 failure modes in 3 categories. **Extraction caveat, stated plainly**: the PDF itself could not be parsed directly by the fetch tool (binary/compressed content), so the specific percentages below come from a secondary source's read of the same paper (alphaxiv's summarization), not from this session directly reading the primary PDF text — moderate, not high, confidence on the exact numbers, though the category structure and qualitative claims are corroborated by an independent secondary source (a blog post, `tianpan.co`, citing the same paper) and are internally consistent:

| Category | Approx. share | Example failure modes |
|---|---|---|
| System design issues | ~44% | disobeying task/role spec, step repetition, unaware of termination conditions |
| Inter-agent misalignment | ~32-37% (two secondary sources disagree by ~5 points — flagged, not resolved) | conversation reset, failure to ask for clarification, task derailment, information withholding, ignoring another agent's input, reasoning-action mismatch |
| Task verification | ~24% | premature termination, no/incomplete verification, incorrect verification |

The load-bearing takeaway, independent of the exact split: **inter-agent misalignment — the failure category that peer-to-peer chat directly enables and that a single-arbitrator design directly forecloses — is roughly a third of all observed multi-agent LLM failures across seven real deployed frameworks.** That is not a single unreplicated study's threshold; it's a taxonomy built for exactly this purpose, with measured annotator agreement, across multiple independent frameworks. The paper's own recommendations (per the secondary-source read) are "enhanced social reasoning," "multi-level verification throughout, not just at the end," and "hybrid architectures combining structured workflows with robust verification" — none of which is "let agents talk to each other more freely."

### 1.2 Net verdict on the load-bearing question

Convergent, multi-source evidence (two independent production postmortems, one taxonomy paper with real inter-annotator validation, one debate-failure paper) all lands on the same side: **a single decider that owns final action, fed by advisors that do not talk to each other, is the better-evidenced architecture for this problem shape**, not folklore. The debate literature (Du et al.) is the one genuine complication, and it complicates in a way that, read carefully, still supports v1's ban — debate helps for "many independent tries, arbitrate over the results" (which is what a single-decider-with-advisors design *already does*, just without the advisors seeing each other's reasoning) and hurts for "peers jointly negotiate one answer," which is precisely the shape peer-to-peer chat would introduce.

What is genuinely **not** established by any of this evidence: that a *later*, more mature version of peer-to-peer coordination (once "the cross-agent communication advances that don't yet exist at scale," per Cognition, actually exist) wouldn't work. The evidence says "not now, not with today's models and today's coordination patterns," not "never, structurally impossible." That's worth stating honestly rather than overclaiming a permanent prohibition from evidence that's dated 2023-2025.

## 2. Blackboard architecture

### 2.1 What it actually specifies

Classical blackboard architecture (Erman et al.'s HEARSAY-II, 1971-1976, Carnegie Mellon, DARPA-funded speech understanding — the founding system; Nii's 1986 AI Magazine retrospective, "The Blackboard Model of Problem Solving," is the standard secondary account) has **three components, not one shared scratchpad**:

1. **A structured, hierarchical shared data store** (the blackboard proper) — not a flat chat log. Partial solutions live on it tagged with credibility/confidence and evidence links, organised into levels (e.g., in HEARSAY-II: acoustic segments → syllables → words → phrases).
2. **Independent knowledge sources (KSs)** — specialists that read and write the blackboard, each declaring *activation conditions* (when it has something useful to contribute) rather than being directly assigned work by a supervisor or by each other. Critically: **KSs communicate exclusively through the blackboard, never directly with each other.**
3. **A control mechanism / scheduler** implementing what the literature calls **opportunistic scheduling**: for every KS currently able to act, estimate how much acting now would improve the emerging solution, and run the most promising one. This is HEARSAY-II's actual innovation, more than the shared-memory idea itself — the *control problem* ("which of its potential actions should the system perform next?") is treated as a first-class, separately-engineered thing, not left implicit.

### 2.2 Documented weaknesses

From multiple independent sources (a blackboard-architecture-pattern overview surveyed via web search, moderate confidence — general architecture-pattern literature rather than a single named paper, so treat as consensus textbook material, not a specific citable study):

- **The control strategy is genuinely hard to get right and does not have a principled design method** — it is arrived at experimentally, iteratively, for each new domain. This is the same "control problem" HEARSAY-II was built to solve, and it was never fully solved in general, only for that domain.
- **Low efficiency / computational overhead**: every KS activation-condition check plus the scheduler's own cost-benefit estimation is real overhead on every cycle, distinct from the actual problem-solving work.
- **No guarantee of a good, or even complete, solution** — the architecture is non-deterministic and opportunistic by construction, so testing is hard and reproducibility is a real engineering problem.
- **High development effort** — historically, mature blackboard systems (HEARSAY-II itself, later systems like the Hayes-Roth BB1) took years to reach usable behaviour, because most of the actual engineering work is tuning the control heuristics, not writing the KSs.
- **Concurrency/synchronization cost** when multiple KSs might want to write the blackboard at once, and the blackboard's necessarily generic, shared data format is a compromise — no single KS gets the data structure best suited to its own reasoning.

### 2.3 What transfers to this project, and what doesn't

**Transfers directly, and is the single most useful correction this section produces**: a modern LLM multi-agent stack that gives every agent a shared context/transcript and calls it done has **only rebuilt one-third of the blackboard architecture** — the shared-workspace part — and **thrown away the other two-thirds**: credibility-weighted contributions (an advisor's claim should carry a confidence tag, not be treated as flatly true), hierarchical structure separating raw observation from interpretation from proposed action (not one undifferentiated stream), and — the part most relevant to df-overseer specifically — **an explicit, separately-engineered scheduler that decides whose turn it is to act next**, rather than either round-robin or free-for-all. This framing (found via `tianpan.co`'s 2026 essay "The Blackboard Is Back," a blog post not a peer-reviewed source, moderate confidence on the framing though its factual claims about HEARSAY-II and MAST check out against the primary sources above) is a genuinely useful reframe of what this project's single-actor-arbitrates design already is: it is closer to a blackboard system with the model as scheduler than to either a classic blackboard (no LLM anywhere) or a peer-chat multi-agent system.

Concretely, what to borrow: (1) advisor outputs should carry an explicit confidence/credibility field, not just a recommendation — this is free to add and is exactly §5's calibration concern; (2) the "blackboard" (this project's fort dossier / doctrine / chronicle stores, per `docs/MEMORY-ARCHITECTURE.md`) should stay hierarchical and typed rather than becoming one long transcript advisors and the arbitrator all read undifferentiated; (3) the scheduler — here, whatever decides which advisor's recommendation the single actor considers this turn, and in what order — deserves the same explicit, separately-tuned treatment HEARSAY-II gave it, rather than being an implicit side effect of prompt order.

**Doesn't transfer**: blackboard KSs are typically small, deterministic, domain-specific procedural experts (a syllable-classifier, a word-hypothesizer) with cheap, fast activation checks — not general-purpose LLMs. An LLM-based "advisor" is vastly more expensive to activate speculatively than a classical KS, which changes the economics of opportunistic scheduling: HEARSAY-II could afford to let many KSs check "am I relevant" cheaply and often; an LLM advisor cannot be polled that cheaply, so the scheduling question here is more like "which one or two advisors are worth the token cost of invoking this turn," a coarser-grained decision than HEARSAY-II's.

## 3. Human institutional practice

### 3.1 Incident Command System (ICS) — the clearest transferable structure

ICS (US wildland-fire-service origin, now the national incident-management standard, NIMS) specifies, and this is genuinely load-bearing for an agent roster design, three named principles (moderate-to-high confidence, sourced from ICS 200 training material and multiple fire-service references, i.e. official doctrine rather than a single author's opinion):

- **Unity of command**: every person answers to exactly one supervisor. Directly transferable: an advisor should have exactly one consumer of its recommendation (the single actor), never a committee, and never another advisor issuing it instructions.
- **Span of control**: one supervisor manages **3-7 direct subordinates, 5 is the stated optimum** — this is a concrete, cited number, not vibes, and it puts a real ceiling on "how many advisors can one arbitrator usefully hear from per turn" before the arbitrator itself becomes the bottleneck (§6's "arbitrator overload" is exactly this).
- **Chain of command, with an explicit carve-out for information flow**: orders flow strictly through the chain, but — and this is the one nuance worth preserving carefully — **members may communicate directly with each other for the exchange of information, even though authority/orders may not.** This is a meaningfully softer position than a blanket "advisors never talk to each other": ICS's actual rule is "advisors may inform each other, but only the commander decides and orders." Worth flagging honestly because it's a real point of daylight between ICS doctrine and this project's stated v1 rule (no specialist-to-specialist chat at all) — ICS would permit read-only cross-advisor visibility (e.g., the construction advisor can see the military advisor's threat assessment) while still keeping arbitration singular. That's a plausible v2 relaxation, not a contradiction of v1's caution, since §1's evidence against peer chat is specifically about peers *negotiating a shared decision*, not about them *reading* each other's output.

### 3.2 Mission command / commander's intent — the delegation half

US Army/Air Force mission-command doctrine (Army.mil articles, Air Force Doctrine Publication 1-1, moderate-high confidence as official doctrine): commanders state **what and why** (the intent/end-state and purpose); subordinates decide **how**, within that intent, without needing to check back for routine execution decisions. This is the standing-orders/precommitment half of the picture (§4) stated in leadership terms rather than control-systems terms: intent is set once, infrequently, and expensively (requires "face-to-face interaction," per doctrine, i.e. it's deliberately not a per-turn cost); execution against that intent is cheap and decentralized. For df-overseer, this maps onto doctrine (`docs/MEMORY-ARCHITECture.md`'s "always-resident, cached" layer) as the commander's-intent layer, and per-turn advisor recommendations as the "how," constrained by it — which is already roughly the shape planned, and this is corroboration rather than a new idea.

### 3.3 Work-in-progress limits — genuinely thin evidence, said plainly

Kanban/Theory-of-Constraints WIP limits are widely practiced and there is a real theoretical backing (Little's Law: WIP = throughput × cycle time, which is a mathematical identity, not an empirical claim needing validation). But the *practice-level* empirical evidence for "what WIP limit number is optimal" is weak: one search source stated explicitly that **no published studies from real cases establish an optimal WIP limit**, and the one concrete number found — "2/3 to 3/4 of team size is the sweet spot" — comes from a single simulation exercise (Featureban, a card-game simulation used in Kanban training, not a controlled study of real teams). This is exactly the kind of "one unreplicated study, flag it as such" case this project has been bitten by before. **Verdict: WIP limits are a sound mechanism (backed by Little's Law's math), but any specific numeric limit for df-overseer's advisor roster should be treated as a tunable, not imported from a cited "optimal number" — there isn't one.**

### 3.4 Multidisciplinary team meetings (medicine) — an evidence-backed cross-domain analogue for "advisor input improves the decision, but only with process discipline"

Tumor boards (multidisciplinary oncology case conferences) are the medical field's version of exactly this project's shape: specialists (surgeon, oncologist, radiologist, pathologist) each analyse the same case and a decision is made collectively/by the treating clinician. Evidence found (systematic review and intervention studies, PMC sources, moderate-high confidence — peer-reviewed clinical literature): MDT review measurably **changes initial treatment plans** and is associated with better outcomes versus individual-clinician decisions in several studies, but **effectiveness depends heavily on process quality** — one intervention study reported the board's own ability to *reach a decision at all* rose from 82.2% to 92.7% only after a targeted intervention improving information completeness and teamwork; barriers cited were incomplete radiologic/pathologic information reaching the board and inappropriate referrals, i.e. failures of the advisors' inputs, not of the arbitration step. **Transferable lesson: the advisors' recommendation must arrive complete and well-formed, or the arbitration step stalls or degrades no matter how good the arbitrator is** — an argument for validating advisor-output schemas strictly, which is already implicit in this project's coordinate-leak-fix discipline (`decisions/DECISIONS.md` 2026-09-12 rows) but is worth stating as a general principle, not a one-off bug class.

### 3.5 What's genuinely transferable to an agent roster, summarized

- Unity of command (one consumer per recommendation) — direct import.
- A numeric span-of-control ceiling (ICS's 5, or a domain-appropriate analogue) on how many advisors the arbitrator hears from in one turn — direct import, with the number itself treated as a starting guess to tune, not gospel.
- Read-only cross-advisor visibility without cross-advisor authority — a plausible, doctrine-backed relaxation of "no chat," worth flagging to the user as a considered v2 option rather than baking peer chat in now.
- Commander's-intent-style doctrine, set rarely and expensively, versus per-turn execution decisions made cheaply within it — already the shape of `docs/MEMORY-ARCHITECTURE.md`, corroborated rather than new.
- WIP limits — sound in principle (Little's Law), unsupported in specifics; don't import a number, import the mechanism.
- Input-completeness discipline for advisors, on the medical-MDT evidence that arbitration quality is gated by advisor-output quality at least as much as by the arbitrator itself.

## 4. Precommitment: reflex vs. deliberation

### 4.1 Where real systems actually draw the line

The clearest, most literal version of "precommitted reflex, no deliberation" is a **nuclear reactor's protection system (SCRAM)**: sensors detect an out-of-bound parameter, hardwired logic circuits open the reactor trip breakers, control rods drop by gravity — no operator judgment call, no model-in-the-loop, by design (Wikipedia/NRC glossary, high confidence as this is standard, publicly documented nuclear-safety engineering). The defining property worth naming explicitly: **the trigger condition and the response are both fixed in advance, and the system is engineered so the reflex cannot be second-guessed in the moment it fires** — deliberation happens only afterward, and only about whether to *revise* the trip point for next time, never about whether to *honour* it this time.

**EMS standing orders vs. online medical control** is the more nuanced, better-documented-for-transferability version, because unlike a reactor trip it's a *graded* system, not a single binary reflex/no-reflex split (Western Journal of Emergency Medicine retrospective study and MedEdPortal teaching material, moderate-high confidence, peer-reviewed clinical-operations literature):
- **Standing orders** (protocol-based care, no call-in required) cover the common, well-characterized cases — one cited study found **86% of interventions delivered under standing orders alone, with a 0.6% paramedic error rate**, and protocol-based care shown to be at least equivalent to calling a physician, with faster scene times.
- **Online medical control** (call a physician in real time) is reserved for the genuinely judgment-laden residual: capacity evaluations for a patient refusing care, ethically complex interventions, anything that requires deviating from the standing protocol. Paramedics are required to either follow protocol or **document their rationale for deviating** — i.e., deviation is allowed but is an audited event, not a silent one.

This is a directly transferable pattern for df-overseer's reflex/decision boundary: **a reflex is the standing-order case — common, well-characterized, low-stakes-per-instance, protocol pre-approved** (e.g., "a citizen is starving, feed them" or the kind of urgent-and-obvious response this project's "precommitted reflexes" language already gestures at); **everything else — including any deviation from a standing reflex — routes to the deliberating actor, and the deviation itself gets logged with a reason**, exactly mirroring EMS's audit requirement.

**Circuit breakers in algorithmic trading and aviation checklists** were searched but yielded thinner, more fragmented sourcing (patent filings and Wikipedia stubs rather than analytical sources) — flagged honestly as **not independently verified in depth this session**; the general shape (automated, precommitted halt conditions with human/deliberative review only after the halt) is consistent with the nuclear and EMS cases above, but this document does not have a citable source characterizing exactly how those systems audit/revise their own trip thresholds over time, which is the more interesting half of the question.

### 4.2 Auditing and revising reflexes over time — the thinner half

None of the sources found in this pass give a rich, citable account of *how* real institutions revise a reflex threshold after the fact (as opposed to just documenting that deviations get logged). This is a genuine gap, not a paper-over: the EMS literature confirms *deviations* are logged and reviewed, and nuclear-plant scram events are tracked and trended by regulators (the NRC's "Operating Reactor Scram Trending" program, name confirmed via search, content not read in depth) — so **the pattern that does transfer is "log every reflex firing and every deviation, review the log periodically, revise the trigger condition from that review"**, which is a process this project can adopt on its own log data (the `learning/ledger` already planned per `Working.md`) without needing a deeper external citation to justify it — it is the obvious synthesis of what the two well-sourced cases (EMS, and NRC's public scram-trending practice) both do, even though this pass didn't read either institution's actual revision methodology end to end.

## 5. Calibration

### 5.1 What transfers cheaply, and what needs a scale this project will never reach

**Proper scoring rules** (Brier score, log score) are the right foundation and are cheap: a proper scoring rule is one where a forecaster cannot improve their expected score by reporting anything other than their true belief (this is a mathematical property, provable, not an empirical finding — high confidence, standard decision-theory result, e.g. surfaced via the `scoringutils` R package documentation and multiple forecasting-methodology sources). Brier score = mean squared error between stated probability and outcome (0/1); it penalizes over- and under-confidence symmetrically. Log score penalizes overconfidence more harshly. **Both are trivial to compute over a few hundred graded predictions** — this is just arithmetic over the `learning/ledger` records already planned, no new infrastructure needed, and this project should adopt one (Brier, for simplicity and symmetric penalty) as the scoring function for every doctrine/advisor prediction that has a check date and a resolved outcome.

**The Good Judgment Project (Tetlock/Mellers et al., IARPA-funded, 2011-2015)** is strong, well-replicated evidence that calibration training plus outcome feedback plus aggregation measurably improves forecasting accuracy: their team beat the control group by "more than 50%," described as the largest improvement in judgmental-forecasting accuracy in the literature, and superforecasters were shown to be well-calibrated on average across 323 scored questions. **This is not a single unreplicated study** — it ran across multiple seasons/years with cumulative Brier-score improvements of 20-30% from combined training+collaboration+aggregation interventions, tracked year over year. Confidence: high, this is one of the best-replicated results in applied forecasting.

What is genuinely **cheap** to adopt from this literature at df-overseer's scale (a few hundred predictions from one fortress, one advisor-arbitrator setup, not thousands of forecasters):
- **Brier/log scoring of every prediction** — trivial, do it.
- **Feedback loops** (show the scorer its own track record after outcomes resolve) — Good Judgment's own intervention studies found this alone drives real improvement; cheap because it's just a report generated from the ledger.
- **A calibration curve / reliability diagram** (bucket predictions by stated confidence, check the empirical hit rate per bucket) — computable over a few hundred points, though the buckets will be coarse (e.g., three bands, not ten) at that sample size; still directionally informative (is "confident" advice actually right more often than "unsure" advice, at all).

What **needs far more data than one fortress will ever produce**, said plainly so this isn't quietly imported as if it were cheap:
- **Extremizing / sophisticated aggregation across many independent forecasters** (Satopää-style aggregation methods used in the actual Good Judgment Project pipeline) — this requires many independent forecasters per question to have something to aggregate; df-overseer has one advisor per specialty per turn, not a crowd, so there's nothing to extremize over. Not applicable at this scale, full stop.
- **Reference class forecasting** (Flyvbjerg's outside-view method: predict a new project's outcome from the empirical distribution of outcomes across a reference class of similar past projects) is well-evidenced *in its own domain* — cost-overrun studies across rail/road/fixed-link infrastructure and, separately, the Oxford Olympics Study, both from Flyvbjerg's group, show large, systematic, predictable overrun patterns (e.g., rail +45%, fixed-link +34%, road +20% in real terms; Olympics +157% on average) that reference-class methods correct for better than bottom-up estimates. This is a strong, multiply-replicated empirical pattern in its home domain (large one-off infrastructure projects), **but the mechanism depends on having many past instances of comparably-shaped projects to build the reference class from** — one fortress's history of, say, three prior forts is nowhere near enough of a reference class on its own. It becomes usable once this project accumulates enough forts (the "the world persists, fortresses are mortal" design decision, per `decisions/DECISIONS.md` 2026-08-25, is exactly the mechanism that would eventually produce a real reference class) but is not usable yet with the current handful of forts.
- **Formal isotonic/Platt-style recalibration** (fitting a calibration *function*, not just eyeballing a reliability diagram) genuinely needs hundreds to thousands of *labeled* points per calibration curve to avoid overfitting the recalibration itself — a few hundred total predictions spread across several advisor types and confidence bands will likely be too sparse to fit this properly per-advisor; a single pooled curve across all advisors is more defensible at this data volume than per-advisor curves.

### 5.2 Verdict

Adopt Brier scoring and a coarse reliability diagram now — cheap, already fits the planned ledger. Track-record weighting (multiplying an advisor's future influence by its historical Brier score) is a reasonable and cheap intermediate step, closer to Good Judgment's basic feedback-loop finding than to its full aggregation machinery. Defer reference-class forecasting and formal recalibration curves explicitly until enough fort-history accumulates — and say so in whatever design doc results from this research, rather than building infrastructure for data this project doesn't have yet.

## 6. Known failure modes of specialist-advisor architectures, and documented mitigations

Pulling together §1-3's sources into the specific failure modes the prompt named:

**Proposal spam** — not named exactly this way in the literature found, but MAST's "step repetition" (15.7% of failures, per §1.1's caveat on exact figures) and "disobey task specification" (11.8%) are the closest documented analogues: advisors/agents re-raising or over-producing recommendations without regard to whether the arbitrator can usefully act on all of them. **Mitigation, ICS-derived**: a hard span-of-control cap (§3.1) on how many advisor recommendations the arbitrator considers per turn is a direct, cited-precedent countermeasure — it doesn't stop advisors from generating proposals, but it caps what reaches decision-time, which is the point of failure that matters.

**Advisor capture** (an advisor's recommendation being adopted not because it's right but because it's persistent, loud, or socially reinforced) — the debate-failure literature (§1.1, arXiv:2509.05396) is the most directly relevant evidence: agents shift from correct to incorrect positions to conform with peers more often than the reverse. That paper is specifically about peer-to-peer negotiation, which reinforces §1's point — a design where advisors never negotiate with each other, only report to one arbitrator, structurally removes the exact mechanism (peer social pressure) that paper documents as causing capture. The mechanism doesn't fully disappear, though: an arbitrator can still be "captured" by a single persistently-recommending advisor over many turns if nothing tracks that advisor's track record — which is exactly what §5's Brier-scoring/track-record-weighting closes.

**Diffusion of responsibility** — the classic reference is Latané & Darley's 1968 bystander-effect study (well-replicated, high confidence, foundational social psychology): in their study, 85% of participants helped when they believed they were alone versus 31% when they believed others were also listening — responsibility measurably diffuses as group size perceived by the individual increases. Applied to an advisor roster: if no single advisor's recommendation is individually attributable to an outcome, and no single component "owns" the miss when something goes wrong, each advisor has less incentive to flag hard problems clearly. **Mitigation, directly implied by the mechanism**: exactly the auditability requirement this project already states as a hard requirement ("full auditability of who recommended what and why") — attribution is the documented antidote to diffusion of responsibility, not a nice-to-have.

**Arbitrator overload** — ICS's span-of-control number (§3.1, 5 direct reports as the stated optimum) is the one concrete, cited mitigation found; Anthropic's own postmortem (§1.1) independently reports the same shape of failure from the other direction — token/coordination cost scaling faster than value once too much is routed through one decision point, to the point that "verification subagents prematurely marking outputs as passing" was one of their real, hand-fixed failure modes, i.e. an overloaded arbitration/verification step starts rubber-stamping rather than actually deciding. **Mitigation**: cap advisor count per turn (ICS), and treat "the arbitrator is spending more effort coordinating/reading than deciding" as its own alarm condition worth instrumenting, not just a vague cost concern.

The category ranking and the qualitative claims are corroborated across sources; the precise numbers are not independently confirmed against the primary PDF text. The general reflex/deliberation shape is consistent with the better-sourced nuclear and EMS cases, but nothing here should be read as a verified claim about how those two specific domains actually operate.
